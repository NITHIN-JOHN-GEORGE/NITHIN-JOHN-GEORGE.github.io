In [Part 1](/blog/cgroups-deep-dive-kubernetes-part-1/) we built the foundations. In short:

- A **cgroup** is a folder under `/sys/fs/cgroup`, and its files are limits the kernel enforces.
- The kubelet turns `requests.cpu` into `cpu.weight` and `limits.cpu` into `cpu.max`, for every pod, in a tree of cgroups (one box per pod, one per container).
- **CPU** can be made to wait. **Memory** can't be handed back the same way, so it fails differently ([Part 3](/blog/cgroups-deep-dive-kubernetes-part-3/) covers how).

Part 1 opened with an outage: a service throttled in about 40% of its CPU periods while its CPU graph showed plenty of headroom, until raising its limit made the restarts stop. This part explains exactly how that happens.

> [!TLDR]
> - A CPU **request** becomes a weight: your share of CPU when pods compete. When CPUs are idle, it does nothing.
> - A CPU **limit** becomes `cpu.max`: a budget per 100 ms, shared by all your threads. Spend it early and the whole container waits. That's throttling, and it can happen at low average CPU.
> - Throttling slows the work behind HTTP and exec health checks too, which can turn into restarts (liveness) and pods dropping out of Services (readiness).
> - In production, watch the throttle ratio, `nr_throttled / nr_periods`, and CPU pressure, not average CPU.
> - Waiting for CPU isn't always your limit: if you're waiting but not throttled, look at your neighbours, your requests and the node.

## Sharing a CPU

### 12. How Linux shares a CPU

*Question: when more threads want to run than there are cores, who goes next?*

This is the part most posts skip, and it's what makes CPU requests and limits make sense.

A core runs one thread at a time ([chapter 1](/blog/cgroups-deep-dive-kubernetes-part-1/#1-what-happens-when-you-run-a-program)). Every thread that's ready to run waits in a queue, and the kernel's scheduler keeps picking the next one. For years that scheduler was called **CFS**, the Completely Fair Scheduler. That's the `cfs` you'll see in metric names like `container_cpu_cfs_throttled_periods_total`. The rule it uses is simple to state: **keep a running tally of how much CPU each thread has had, and give the next turn to whoever has had the least.**

That tally is called **virtual runtime**, and the word *virtual* is where weights come in. A thread with a higher weight has its tally grow more slowly. Run it for 10 ms with double the weight, and its tally only goes up by 5. So it keeps coming out as "had the least" more often, and ends up with roughly twice the CPU. Weight doesn't reserve anything; it just changes how fast your tally fills up.

Treat this as a mental model: it's the right way to think about weights, but today's kernels pick the next thread with a slightly more precise rule, in the box below.

cgroups plug straight into this. A cgroup is scheduled like one big thread with its own weight (`cpu.weight`), competing with its sibling cgroups. Whatever time the cgroup wins is then shared out among the threads inside it, the same way. That's how a pod's request turns into a share of the node's CPU.

There are two separate mechanisms, and they map neatly onto requests and limits:

| Mechanism | File | Comes from | When it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Weight** (sharing) | `cpu.weight` | `requests.cpu` | Only when cgroups compete for the same CPUs |
| **Bandwidth** (capping) | `cpu.max` | `limits.cpu` | Always, even on an idle node |

Chapter 13 is about the first, chapter 14 about the second.

> [!DEEPER]
> **CFS or EEVDF?**
>
> For years this scheduler was called **CFS**, the Completely Fair Scheduler, and most articles still use that name. With Linux 6.6 (2023), Linux began moving to **EEVDF** ("earliest eligible virtual deadline first"). It keeps the same virtual runtime and weights, but the rule for who goes next is more precise: among the threads that are **eligible** (those that haven't had more than their fair share), it runs the one with the **earliest virtual deadline**. That lets short, latency-sensitive tasks get their turn sooner. For everything in this series nothing changes: `cpu.weight`, `cpu.max` and throttling work the same way under both. The old name lives on in places like the metric `container_cpu_cfs_throttled_periods_total`.

### 13. CPU requests become weights

*Question: what does a CPU request actually get you?*

A request does **not** reserve a core. When the node is quiet it does nothing at all: anyone can use spare CPU. It only matters when everyone is hungry at once, and then the kernel cuts the pizza in proportion to each group's `cpu.weight`.

To see who wins, measure how much CPU each pod uses in 10 seconds. This reads each pod's CPU counter twice and prints the difference in millicores:

```bash
l1=$(kubectl -n lab exec weight-low  -- awk '/usage_usec/{print $2}' /sys/fs/cgroup/cpu.stat)
h1=$(kubectl -n lab exec weight-high -- awk '/usage_usec/{print $2}' /sys/fs/cgroup/cpu.stat)
sleep 10
l2=$(kubectl -n lab exec weight-low  -- awk '/usage_usec/{print $2}' /sys/fs/cgroup/cpu.stat)
h2=$(kubectl -n lab exec weight-high -- awk '/usage_usec/{print $2}' /sys/fs/cgroup/cpu.stat)
echo "weight-low: $(( (l2 - l1) / 10000 ))m   weight-high: $(( (h2 - h1) / 10000 ))m"
```

```
weight-low: 1060m   weight-high: 9103m
```

`weight-high` requested `800m`, eight times the `100m` that `weight-low` requested, and it got about **8.6× more CPU**: 9103m against 1060m. Together they used the whole 10-CPU node. **When everyone is busy, CPU is split in proportion to the requests.** Anything else busy on the node takes its share too, so treat the exact numbers as a measurement, not a promise.

> [!DEEPER]
> **How a request becomes a weight**
>
> The kernel doesn't know what millicores are. Kubernetes first turns your request into **CPU shares** (millicores × 1024 ÷ 1000; that's the `shares: 256` in [chapter 10](/blog/cgroups-deep-dive-kubernetes-part-1/#10-follow-one-pod-down-to-the-kernel)), and shares are then turned into a `cpu.weight`. Two things are worth knowing.
>
> **1. Every pod gets two weights, not one.** The pod has its own cgroup, and each container has one inside it ([chapter 11](/blog/cgroups-deep-dive-kubernetes-part-1/#11-where-every-pod-lives-the-cgroup-tree)). Each gets a `cpu.weight`, written by a different program with a different formula. A weight is just a number from 1 to 10,000, with no unit. On its own it means nothing; only how it compares with its neighbours matters. 32 next to 4 means "eight times as much". For our two pods:
>
> | `cpu.weight` of | `weight-low` `100m` | `weight-high` `800m` | Written by | Decides |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Pod's cgroup | 4 | 32 | the kubelet | how pods share the node |
> | Container's cgroup | 17 | 84 | the runtime | how containers share their pod |
>
> **2. Pods compete at the pod level.** So the split you measured follows 4 and 32: exactly 8×, like the requests. The container weights, 17 and 84, only matter between containers inside the same pod. (The 35 in [chapter 10](/blog/cgroups-deep-dive-kubernetes-part-1/#10-follow-one-pod-down-to-the-kernel) was a container's weight too.) To predict how pods will share a busy node, read the **pod** cgroup's `cpu.weight`. Lab 13 prints both.
>
> *Why are the container numbers different?* The kubelet uses a straight-line formula that gives small weights: even a whole CPU comes out at only 39, below the default of 100. Container runtimes recently switched to a curve that puts one CPU at about 100.

> [!KEY]
> A CPU request is your share of CPU when groups compete. When CPUs are idle, it does nothing.

## Limits and throttling

### 14. CPU limits become a budget: throttling

*Question: what happens when a container hits its CPU limit?*

A limit is a different mechanism altogether: not a share, but a hard budget. Think of `cpu.max` as a **wallet that's refilled every 100 milliseconds**. With `limits.cpu: 200m`, the wallet gets 20 ms of CPU time per 100 ms *period*. Spend it early, and you wait until the next refill, even if the machine has nine idle CPUs.

Here's the trap, and it's the one I see most often: **the wallet is shared by all threads in the cgroup.** Four busy threads running on four CPUs spend 20 ms in about 5 ms of real time. After that, every thread in the group that's ready to run has to wait until the next period, about 95 ms later in this example. Your dashboard says "20% CPU", but every request that lands in one of those gaps is slow. That waiting is **throttling**.

- At the start of each period, the cgroup's wallet is filled with its quota (20 ms here).
- Each CPU that's running one of the cgroup's threads takes a small chunk out of the wallet (5 ms by default), spends it, then comes back for more. This chunk is only how the budget is handed out to each CPU. It isn't how long a thread runs before the scheduler switches to another one.
- When the wallet is empty, every CPU running that cgroup's threads stops running them, until a timer refills the wallet at the next period.

That's why the number of threads matters so much: four CPUs drain the wallet four times as fast as one.

This wallet has a name: the **CFS bandwidth controller**, the part of the Linux scheduler that enforces `cpu.max`. It was built for CFS, the scheduler from chapter 12. When Linux began moving from CFS to EEVDF in 6.6, the bandwidth controller and its files stayed exactly the same. That's why the throttling metrics you'll see in production still have `cfs` in their names, like `container_cpu_cfs_throttled_periods_total`.

> [!DEEPER]
> **Two details that matter in production**
>
> - **`nr_periods` only counts busy periods.** It goes up only when the cgroup had work to do. So "40% throttled" means 40% of the periods when the service was actually doing something, not 40% of the day.
> - **`cpu.max.burst`.** Since Linux 5.14, a cgroup can carry unused quota forward and spend it in a burst later, up to the amount in `cpu.max.burst`. It suits apps that are idle most of the time and spiky occasionally. Kubernetes has no field for it today, but it's worth knowing the kernel has it.

**Back to the outage from [Part 1's intro](/blog/cgroups-deep-dive-kubernetes-part-1/), one symptom at a time.** How did a pod at "80% of request" end up restarting?

#### Why the CPU graph looked healthy

Three things hid the problem:

- **We compared usage with the wrong number.** "Below 80% of request" measures against `cpu.weight`, which never throttles anything. Throttling only comes from `cpu.max`, the limit.
- **Averages hide bursts.** A one-minute CPU graph averages 600 periods. Throttling happens inside a single 100 ms period: a burst of threads empties the wallet in a few milliseconds, then everything waits. A pod can be quiet most of the time and still hit the wall whenever a burst arrives.
- **"40% throttled" was 40% of busy periods** (chapter 14), so it was even worse than it looked during traffic.

A quick worked example with request = 2 cores and limit = 5 cores. Suppose that in 25% of periods a burst spends the full 500 ms wallet, and in the rest the pod is fairly quiet at about 40 ms:

```
average = 0.25 × 5 cores + 0.75 × 0.4 cores = 1.55 cores
        → about 78% of a 2-core request: the dashboard says "fine"
        → but throttled in 25% of periods
```

#### Why throttling broke the health probes

Throttling doesn't touch the kubelet. What it slows down is the work **inside your container**, and whether that breaks a probe depends on how the probe checks:

- **HTTP and gRPC probes** need your app to answer a request. If the app's threads are waiting for the next period, the answer waits too.
- **Exec probes** run a command inside the container. That command is in the same cgroup, so it waits for the same budget.
- **TCP probes** only check that a connection to the port can be opened. The kernel accepts connections on the app's behalf, so a throttled app usually still passes. A TCP probe won't catch this kind of trouble.

Lab 15's service, like the one in our outage, used HTTP probes. What the kubelet does when a probe keeps failing depends on the probe:

| Probe | Question it asks | When it keeps failing |
|---|---|---|
| **Liveness** | "Is this process stuck?" | The kubelet **kills** the container. Its `restartPolicy` decides what happens next; for a Deployment that's always a restart |
| **Readiness** | "Can this pod take traffic right now?" | The pod is marked **not ready** and removed from the Service's endpoints, so it stops getting traffic. Nothing is killed |
| **Startup** | "Has the app finished booting?" | Like liveness: the container is killed and its `restartPolicy` applies. Liveness and readiness checks wait until it passes |

An HTTP probe is a request with a deadline (`timeoutSeconds`, 1 second by default). A single freeze of a few tens of milliseconds won't break that, but the effects stack up:

1. **CPU time stretches into much more real time.** If the container's threads can only run for the first 25 ms of each 100 ms period, a request that needs 100 ms of CPU takes about 400 ms to finish.
2. **Queues fill up.** Slow requests hold on to worker threads, and new requests, including the probe, wait behind them.
3. **The probe misses its deadline** `failureThreshold` times in a row (3 by default).

**Liveness failure: the restart loop.** The container is killed and, under a Deployment, restarted. Warming up (loading caches, opening connection pools, compiling code) is often the most parallel work a service ever does, so a fresh container throttles hardest of all.

#### Why raising the limit fixed it

Going from 5 cores to 8 grew the wallet from 500 ms to 800 ms per period. Normal bursts now finished before it ran dry, so `nr_throttled` stopped climbing. We still saw a little throttling during warmup, which fits: warmup runs more threads in parallel than even 8 cores of budget covers.

#### Why it started "out of nowhere"

Throttling depends on **how many threads want to run at once**, and that can change without anyone changing code:

- **Bigger nodes.** A container can see every core on the host. Modern runtimes (JDK 10+, Go 1.25+) size their thread pools from the CPU limit, but custom pools, native libraries and older runtimes often count host cores. Move to bigger nodes and the same service starts more threads against the same wallet.
- **Burstier traffic** at the same daily volume: synchronized retries, fan-out from callers, cron-driven clients.

We never confirmed the exact trigger, which is common, and it's why the throttling ratio belongs on a dashboard before you need it.

#### What I check now

The throttle ratio, per pod. Treat it as a **signal to investigate**, not a health score on its own: a batch job can be throttled all day and be perfectly fine. What matters is throttling **together with** slow responses, failing probes or high CPU pressure:

```promql
sum by (pod) (rate(container_cpu_cfs_throttled_periods_total[5m]))
/
sum by (pod) (rate(container_cpu_cfs_periods_total[5m]))
```

How many seconds per second a pod spends frozen:

```promql
rate(container_cpu_cfs_throttled_seconds_total[5m])
```

And, when the image has `ps`, how many threads want a CPU right now (`R` means running or waiting to run):

```bash
kubectl exec <pod> -- sh -c 'for i in $(seq 20); do ps -eLo stat | grep -c ^R; sleep 0.2; done'
```

If that number regularly goes above your CPU limit in cores, the wallet is being drained. The lasting fix is to size thread pools and workers to the limit (or the request, if you drop the limit); raising the limit, like we did, buys time.

And for the probes:

- **Keep liveness cheap and local** ("is the process responsive?", not "are my dependencies up?"), and give it a more generous `timeoutSeconds` and `failureThreshold` than readiness, so a short throttling episode doesn't kill the pod.
- **Use a `startupProbe` for slow warmups**, so liveness can't kill a pod that's only slow because it's throttled while booting.
- **Watch for readiness churn** (pods flipping between ready and not ready) next to the throttle ratio. Churn with no restarts is the quiet version of this outage.

**Try it yourself.** [Lab 15](https://github.com/NITHIN-JOHN-GEORGE/kubernetes-resources-under-the-hood/tree/main/labs/15-the-outage) recreates this outage on a laptop: three replicas of a small web service with a 500m CPU request and limit and liveness and readiness probes, hit by bursts of requests. On my run, a pod's average CPU came out at **384m against a 500m request: 77%**, the same "below 80% of request" picture as our outage. Yet the kernel had throttled it in **790 of 843 periods**. Within 90 seconds the slowest requests took over 4 seconds, and every pod had restarted two or three times. Raising the limit to 2 CPUs brought restarts to zero and the slowest request down to about half a second.

#### Should you even set CPU limits?

**What the lab shows.** In lab 15, raising the CPU limit to 2 CPUs took restarts from two or three per pod to none, and the slowest request from over 4 seconds to about half a second. With no limit at all, `cpu.max` reads `max`: there's no budget to run out of, so nothing is throttled.

**What I'd recommend.** For many latency-sensitive services: set honest CPU requests and leave the CPU limit off. You don't give up your fair share: when CPUs are busy, `cpu.weight` still divides them in proportion to requests. What you give up is the forced idling, where a limit throttles you even though the node has CPU to spare.

That's a judgement, not a rule. Leaving limits off has real trade-offs:

- **Isolation.** With untrusted or multi-tenant workloads, you may want a hard cap on what any one of them can burn.
- **Noisy neighbours.** Without limits, pods soak up spare CPU, and how much is spare changes all the time. Your service may be fast on a quiet node and slower on a busy one, which can also make load tests look better than production. ([Waiting for CPU](#waiting-for-cpu-your-limit-or-contention), below, shows what this looks like and how to tell it apart from throttling.)
- **Concurrency control.** A limit caps how much parallel work a service can do at once, which some teams rely on to protect a database or a downstream service.
- **Guaranteed QoS.** Exclusive CPU cores with the static CPU manager need limits equal to requests.
- **Batch jobs** you deliberately want capped.

Keep in mind what a limit does and doesn't do: it **caps** usage. It doesn't guarantee a minimum (that's the request's job), and it doesn't protect a pod from contention with its neighbours. Many manifests carry CPU limits only because they were copied from somewhere else. Whichever way you go, measure throttling and latency before and after the change, on your own workload.

Two things don't change. **Always set memory limits**: memory can't be shared out like CPU, and one leak can take down the node (Part 3). And if you drop the CPU limit, tell your runtime how many cores to use: Go and the JVM size their thread pools from the limit, so without one they see every core on the host. Set `GOMAXPROCS` or `-XX:ActiveProcessorCount` to match your request.

If you want both sides argued in depth, [learnk8s on CPU requests and limits](https://itnext.io/cpu-limits-and-requests-in-kubernetes-fa9d55948b7c) and [Why you should keep using CPU limits](https://dnastacio.medium.com/why-you-should-keep-using-cpu-limits-on-kubernetes-60c4e50dfc61) are good reads.

> Throttling hides behind averages, slows your app's health checks along with everything else, and comes from concurrency, not usage. Watch the ratio, next to latency.

### Waiting for CPU: your limit or contention?

*Question: my pod is slow because it's waiting for CPU. Is it hitting its own limit, or competing with other workloads?*

A thread can be ready to run but unable to get CPU for two different reasons:

- **Throttled by its own limit.** The cgroup has used its CPU quota for the current period. For example, a `500m` limit allows 50 ms of CPU time per 100 ms period. Once that quota is used, runnable work in the cgroup is throttled until quota becomes available again, even if other cores are idle. That's CPU throttling (chapter 14).
- **Waiting for an available CPU.** The cgroup still has quota, but the CPUs it can run on are busy. That's CPU contention: other workloads are using the available CPU time.

The symptoms can look similar, but the causes differ. Check `cpu.stat` for throttling and `cpu.pressure` for time spent waiting for CPU. A pod's CPU pressure can include both kinds of waiting.

#### How to tell which one

You don't have to guess. Compare two metrics from your container:

- **Throttled (`cpu.stat`):** how often your container's hard limit stopped it. (Measures *only* throttling.)
- **Waiting (`cpu.pressure`):** how long your tasks spent stuck waiting for an available CPU core, for *any* reason.

| What you see | Root cause | Where to look |
|---|---|---|
| **Waiting + throttled** | Your limit is too low | Look at your pod's `resources.limits.cpu` |
| **Waiting + not throttled** | The node is crowded | Look at what else is scheduled on your node |

> [!KEY]
> Waiting without throttling means the node is overcrowded by your neighbours: your own limit isn't the problem.

#### How a busy node slows your pod

A pod can wait for CPU even when it has no CPU limit. That happens when other workloads are using the available cores. For example:

1. Batch jobs have large CPU limits, so they're allowed to use several cores.
2. They have small CPU requests, so the scheduler thinks they need little CPU when placing them.
3. Several jobs land on the same node. When they all become busy, they compete with nearby services for CPU time.

The kernel still uses `cpu.weight` to share CPU fairly among runnable groups. But weight doesn't create more CPU: when many threads are runnable, each may spend more time waiting for a turn. This is CPU contention, not throttling. Check `cpu.pressure` for waiting and `cpu.stat` for quota throttling.

**What to do:** set requests that reflect the workload's expected CPU needs. If batch jobs can create unacceptable contention, limit their concurrency or schedule them on separate nodes from latency-sensitive services.

*Lab:* [incident 4](https://github.com/NITHIN-JOHN-GEORGE/kubernetes-resources-under-the-hood/tree/main/incidents/4-waiting-without-throttling) recreates this scenario and lets you change the setup: `make incident-4`.

#### When you raise a CPU limit, revisit the request

Raising a CPU limit gives a pod permission to use more CPU time. It doesn't reserve CPU for that pod or make room for it on the node. The scheduler places pods using their requests, not their limits ([Part 1, chapter 9](/blog/cgroups-deep-dive-kubernetes-part-1/#9-the-journey-of-your-yaml)). If the pod needs more CPU consistently, raise its request too and check that the node has enough allocatable CPU. Otherwise, the pod may take more CPU when it's available, increasing contention for other workloads.

> [!KEY]
> A limit sets a ceiling; a request tells the scheduler how much CPU to plan for.

#### How much CPU pressure is too much?

There's no universal PSI threshold. The `some` value tells you the percentage of time at least one non-idle task in the pod was stalled; it does not tell you how much slower requests became.

Start by comparing `cpu.pressure` with request latency, traffic, and throttling over the same period. A sustained rise in CPU pressure alongside worse latency is a reason to investigate. Treat 5% or 10% as possible investigation triggers for your service, not as pass/fail limits. Use the 1- or 5-minute averages to see sustained pressure; the 10-second average is more useful for spotting short bursts.

## In production

### Watching CPU in production

*Question: I can't `cat` files on production nodes all day. What do I watch instead?*

The good news: every CPU file in this part already has a metric. cAdvisor, built into the kubelet, reads the cgroup files and exports them to Prometheus:

| Kernel file | What it tells you | Prometheus metric |
|---|---|---|
| `cpu.stat` → `usage_usec` | CPU time used | `container_cpu_usage_seconds_total` |
| `cpu.stat` → `nr_periods` | Periods with work to do | `container_cpu_cfs_periods_total` |
| `cpu.stat` → `nr_throttled` | Periods throttled | `container_cpu_cfs_throttled_periods_total` |
| `cpu.stat` → `throttled_usec` | Time spent throttled | `container_cpu_cfs_throttled_seconds_total` |

The one to alert on is the throttle ratio from [chapter 14](#14-cpu-limits-become-a-budget-throttling), the share of periods in which the container ran out of budget:

```
rate(container_cpu_cfs_throttled_periods_total[5m])
  / rate(container_cpu_cfs_periods_total[5m])
```

Our outage would have shown about 0.4 here, while average CPU looked fine. Like any single number, it's a reason to look closer, not a verdict: check it next to latency and probe failures.

#### Pressure: how long tasks wait

Usage tells you how much CPU a workload used. **Pressure stall information (PSI)** tells you how much time tasks lost while waiting for a resource.

Imagine a shop with one checkout. The till can be busy all day, whether one customer or a long queue kept it busy. Usage tells you how busy the till was; pressure tells you how much time customers spent waiting.

For CPU, Linux reports pressure in `cpu.pressure` for a cgroup and in `/proc/pressure/cpu` for the whole node.

To see it, the lab has a pod built to wait. `psi-cpu` has **no CPU limit**, so it's never throttled, but it runs **twice as many busy threads as the node has CPUs**, so its threads have to take turns. Start it, give it 30 seconds, and read its pressure file. (The same file also starts `psi-memory`, which Part 3 uses.)

```bash
kubectl -n lab apply -f labs/19-metrics-and-psi/pods.yaml
kubectl -n lab wait --for=condition=Ready pod/psi-cpu
sleep 30
kubectl -n lab exec psi-cpu -- cat /sys/fs/cgroup/cpu.pressure
```

```
some avg10=59.96 avg60=24.36 avg300=6.05 total=18884381
full avg10=3.74 avg60=1.52 avg300=0.38 total=1359356
```

Start with the first number, `some avg10=59.96`. It means: **for about 60% of the last 10 seconds, at least one of the pod's threads was waiting for a CPU.** That's the number most people need.

The rest of the output, briefly:

- `avg60` and `avg300` are the same thing over the last 1 and 5 minutes. They're lower here because the pod had only been busy for about 30 seconds.
- `total` is all the waiting so far, in microseconds.
- The `full` line counts the time when **all** of the pod's threads were waiting at once. It's rarer, and for CPU you'll mostly look at `some`.

That's the second kind of CPU waiting from ["Waiting for CPU: your limit or contention?"](#waiting-for-cpu-your-limit-or-contention). PSI describes time tasks spend stalled; `cpu.stat` tells you whether the cgroup is being throttled by its quota. Use both to investigate CPU delays.

Since Kubernetes 1.36, the kubelet exposes PSI metrics for nodes, pods, and containers through its Summary API and `/metrics/cadvisor` endpoint. You can graph the pod's waiting time alongside the throttle ratio and node-level CPU pressure. For example:

```promql
rate(container_pressure_cpu_waiting_seconds_total[5m])
```

From node-exporter, the corresponding node-level query is:

```promql
rate(node_pressure_cpu_waiting_seconds_total[5m])
```
